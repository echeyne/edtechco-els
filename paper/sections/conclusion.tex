\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

US early learning standards are public policy that software cannot read. We have
shown that the obstacle is not text recovery but hierarchy recovery, and that an
LLM can recover the hierarchy by classifying each element's nesting position
against a depth map inferred per document, rather than by recognizing label
vocabulary that no two states share. Across four development states and two held
out entirely, detector recall is 1.000 at every level, a manual audit of all 297
in-scope detections finds one hallucinated element, and parsing resolves those
elements into a single canonical schema with no identifier collisions. Ablating
the depth map degrades exactly the levels whose identity is positional and
leaves the others untouched; a rule-based extractor matches the method at a
document's top division and collapses beneath it. The system carries no
per-state rule, which is what allows a new state to be added without new code.

The most valuable immediate extension is also the cheapest. Kentucky's golden is
detection-exhaustive, so grading it inside a trimmed-tier run would yield
quality-at-scale evidence without any new annotation --- the one measurement
this work most conspicuously lacks. Beyond that, the corpus is six states of
more than fifty, and the obvious direction is breadth: each state added tests
generalization again, and enough of them would constitute the national
machine-readable dataset that does not currently exist.

Two further directions follow from the artifact rather than the method. The
normalized store is already served through a browsing and verification
interface; exposing it through a tool-callable interface would let external
applications and agents query standards directly, which is the form in which
curriculum and planning tools actually need them. And the human verification
state the schema carries per element invites a targeted-review question we have
deliberately not answered here: given that confidence is uninformative
(\S\ref{sec:experiments-confidence}), what \emph{would} identify the records
most worth an expert's attention? Our stability measurements suggest one
candidate --- disagreement across repeated runs is cheap to compute and, unlike
confidence, is concentrated in exactly the places where our own defects live.
