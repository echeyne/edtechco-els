\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Document structure and layout analysis}

Recovering logical structure from visually formatted documents is a
long-standing problem. Pipelines for scholarly documents such as
GROBID~\citep{lopez2009grobid} established the pattern of segmenting a PDF
into typed structural units, and large layout datasets such as
PubLayNet~\citep{zhong2019publaynet} enabled supervised page-object
detectors. Layout-aware pretrained encoders --- LayoutLM and its
successors~\citep{xu2020layoutlm,huang2022layoutlmv3} --- and OCR-free
vision--encoder--decoder models such as Donut~\citep{kim2022donut} and
Nougat~\citep{blecher2023nougat} learn joint text--layout representations,
but target flat extraction tasks (classification, key--value extraction,
markup transcription) rather than deep hierarchy recovery.

Closer to our task, a line of work reconstructs a document's
\emph{hierarchical} structure explicitly. DocParser parses complete
document trees from renderings~\citep{rausch2021docparser};
HRDoc~\citep{ma2023hrdoc} and the Comp-HRDoc benchmark with its
Detect-Order-Construct system~\citep{wang2024detect} decompose the problem
into page-object detection, reading-order prediction, and tree
construction; HiPS~\citep{wehnert2025hips} segments deeply nested legal
books using table-of-contents metadata with LLM refinement. All of these
are trained or tuned on corpora with consistent typography --- scholarly
articles or books --- and rely on layout supervision (line-level labels,
TOC metadata, bounding boxes). US early learning standards offer neither:
each of the 50-plus issuing jurisdictions designs its document
independently~\citep{ncecqa_elgs}, so there is no shared typography to
learn, and no annotated corpus exists at any scale. Our detector instead
reasons over OCR-extracted text with an LLM, classifying each element by
its \emph{nesting position} in a per-document depth map rather than by
learned visual features or vocabulary-specific labels. The depth-map pass
is functionally analogous to the table-of-contents subtask of
\citet{wang2024detect}, but is inferred zero-shot from content rather than
predicted by a trained component.

\subsection{LLMs for information extraction}

\citet{brown2020language} established that sufficiently capable language
models perform many tasks from prompts alone, and chain-of-thought
prompting~\citep{wei2022chain} extended this to multi-step structural
reasoning. In-context information extraction has since been demonstrated
in domains where labeled corpora are scarce, notably clinical
text~\citep{agrawal2022large}, and generative LLMs are now competitive
across classic IE tasks~\citep{wadhwa2023revisiting,xu2024large}.
GoLLIE~\citep{sainz2024gollie} showed that models fine-tuned to follow
annotation \emph{guidelines} generalize to unseen extraction schemas; our
prompts operationalize the same insight without fine-tuning, expressing
document-agnostic structural principles (e.g., ``a structural label and
identifier constitute the code; the text after the colon is the title'')
that a frozen commercial model applies to unseen state documents.
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning can outperform in-context learning when
labeled data exists~\citep{liu2022fewshot}; our setting is the complement
of that premise --- no annotated corpus of parsed early learning standards
exists, so the dominant cost of the fine-tuning alternative would be
annotation itself, not compute.

Two further lines of work inform the system design. Constrained decoding
can guarantee schema-valid output~\citep{willard2023efficient}; we instead
validate generated JSON against a typed schema and repair deterministically,
keeping the model API-agnostic. Positional degradation in long
contexts~\citep{liu2024lost} motivates our domain-scoped chunking over
whole-document prompting, and sampling variance at fixed
prompts~\citep{wang2023selfconsistency} motivates the run-to-run stability
measurements we report. Hallucinated content remains a structural risk of
generative extraction~\citep{ji2023survey}; we quantify it directly via a
manual audit of unmatched detections. Closest to our setting as an applied
system, \citet{aggarwal2025fiscal} convert long government fiscal PDFs
into a research database with a multi-stage LLM pipeline, exploiting the
documents' internal arithmetic structure for validation; our pipeline
analogously exploits the internal \emph{hierarchical} consistency of
standards documents (codes as namespace paths, parent--child prefix
relations) to normalize and cross-check extractions.

\subsection{Human-in-the-loop information extraction}

Our pipeline is human-in-the-loop by design: every extracted record is
subject to human verification, tracked per record at all four hierarchy
levels. This follows the machine-teaching paradigm, in which domain
experts retain control of the process rather than merely labeling
data~\citep{mosqueirarey2023human,monarch2021human}, and differs from
active-learning regimes that select \emph{which} items receive human
attention~\citep{settles2009active}: verification here is blanket, and the
detector's confidence scores are recorded but gate nothing. Recent work
positions LLMs as annotators in their own
right~\citep{gilardi2023chatgpt,tan2024large} and studies how to allocate
annotation work between humans and models~\citep{li2023coannotating}; our
division of labor is the draft-and-verify variant, with the model
producing complete structured drafts and humans providing correctness
guarantees the model cannot.

\subsection{NLP for educational standards and curricula}

Machine-readable learning standards have a two-decade history. The
Achievement Standards Network modeled US K--12 standards in RDF to support
resource correlation~\citep{sutton2008achievement}, and the CASE
specification defines an exchange format for competency
frameworks~\citep{1edtech2017case}. Both presuppose that standards are
\emph{already} structured --- ASN was populated by manual modeling, and
CASE is a format, not an extractor. In education research, alignment
between standards, assessments, and instruction is measured by trained
human panels using content-analysis frameworks such as the Surveys of
Enacted Curriculum~\citep{porter2002measuring,polikoff2011howwell};
recent NLP work automates the alignment of assessment items or content
\emph{to} known standards~\citep{xu2025automated}. Across this literature
the standards themselves are assumed available in normalized form. For
early childhood, that assumption fails: unlike K--12, no national
framework spans the age band --- the federal ELOF covers only Head Start
programs~\citep{ohs2015elof} --- and each state publishes its own
guidelines as independently designed PDF
documents~\citep{ncecqa_elgs,ecs2024governance}. The extraction step ---
from heterogeneous PDF to canonical hierarchy --- is the unaddressed
bottleneck this paper automates, and is complementary to the alignment
work above: normalized, hierarchical standards are the input that
cross-state alignment methods require.
