\section{Ethics and Broader Impact}
\label{sec:ethics}

\paragraph{No personal data.} This work processes published policy documents
issued by state education agencies. It involves no student, teacher or family
data, no personally identifying information of any kind, and no human subjects.
The only people named anywhere in the pipeline are the authenticated editors who
verify extracted records, whose identity is bound from their session rather than
supplied by a model.

\paragraph{Stewardship of what the system produces.} Early learning standards
shape curriculum, assessment and teacher preparation, so a misextracted standard
is not a neutral error --- it could propagate into materials that reach
children. This is the reason the system emits a confidence score but gates
nothing on it (\S\ref{sec:method-confidence}), and the reason every record
carries the page of the source PDF it came from: the design assumes a human will
check, and makes checking cheap, rather than assuming the extraction is right.
Our own measurements support that stance. The pipeline is not deterministic, its
stability figures are lower bounds rather than guarantees, and we found a
recorded result of our own that did not reproduce. We would regard deploying
these outputs into anything carrying regulatory or assessment weight without
expert verification as a misuse of them.

\paragraph{Not a substitute for the source.} The normalized corpus is a
navigation aid over documents that remain authoritative. Nothing here
reinterprets a standard, changes its wording, or merges jurisdictions into a
common framework --- the canonical schema records how each state organizes
itself, including where a state uses fewer levels than the schema allows.
Treating a normalized record as the standard, rather than as a pointer into the
document that issues it, would be a misreading of what the system does.

\paragraph{Who benefits, and a caution.} Making fragmented standards queryable
mainly helps those without the resources to do it by hand: small districts,
individual educators, and developers building for them, as against large
publishers who can afford manual normalization. That is the impact we intend.
The corresponding risk is that a machine-readable corpus makes cross-state
comparison easy enough to invite comparisons the documents do not support ---
states differ in what they choose to make explicit, and a state with fewer
recorded indicators has not thereby set a lower bar.
